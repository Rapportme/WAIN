Before You Choose a Marketing Channel, Decide What You Are Trying to Build
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

“Should we do influencer marketing?” “Can we start posting on Instagram?” “Should we run Meta ads?”

These sound like marketing questions. Often, they are questions that arrive too early.

Before choosing a channel, a business needs to decide what it is trying to build in the first place.

A brand may want to be perceived as premium, affordable, trustworthy, fast, expert, friendly, luxurious or simply convenient. Those decisions shape almost every subsequent marketing decision. They influence the audience, the language, the visual identity, the creators a brand works with, the kind of content it produces and even the customers it is willing to attract.

The problem is that channels are now incredibly accessible. Almost every business can run paid ads. Almost every business can create reels. Influencer collaborations are available at many budget levels. A phone can produce content that would once have required a production team.

Access is no longer the difficult part.

Choice is.

A business can easily end up copying the marketing behaviour of competitors. If competitors are using influencers, the natural reaction is to find influencers. If everyone is posting reels, the natural reaction is to post more reels. The campaign may generate reach and engagement while still feeling disconnected from the brand.

That usually happens when the channel becomes the starting point instead of the brand.

Consider an influencer campaign. The creator may have an excellent audience and strong engagement. But if the creator's personality, language and positioning communicate something completely different from what the brand wants to mean, the campaign can create attention without creating the right association.

The same problem exists with content. A business can publish every day and still leave customers unclear about what makes the business different.

Marketing works better when the sequence is reversed.

First: What are we trying to mean?

Then: Who needs to believe it?

Then: What behaviour are we trying to influence?

Only then should the business ask which channels and formats make sense.

The least visible part of marketing is often the most important part. Positioning rarely produces an immediate screenshot. It does not look as exciting as a campaign launch. But it gives the visible work a direction.

There is no shortage of channels.

The harder question is whether the marketing being chosen actually fits the business being built.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 02
What Is the Actual Job in an AI-Assisted Workplace?
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

The question “Will AI take my job?” is becoming increasingly common. But for many roles, there is a more useful question:

What is the actual job?

A job title can describe a position. It does not necessarily describe the value a person creates.

Consider a marketer. If the job is defined as “writing captions”, then an AI tool that can produce captions creates an obvious threat. If the job is defined as understanding customers, developing positioning, deciding what the brand should say, interpreting performance and making better decisions, the role looks very different.

The same distinction appears across functions.

A designer may use AI to generate variations, but the business still needs someone to decide which direction fits the brand. A salesperson may automate follow-ups, but someone still has to understand the customer, handle uncertainty and decide when a conversation needs a human response. An operations professional may automate reporting, but the value is often in noticing what the numbers mean and what needs to change.

AI is particularly good at reducing the amount of manual work inside a job.

That does not automatically mean it replaces the job.

It may instead change where the valuable part of the job sits.

This is an uncomfortable shift because many professional roles have accumulated repetitive tasks over time. People become good at operating tools, preparing formats and following established processes. Those activities can be easier to automate than the underlying judgement.

That makes AI less of a simple replacement story and more of a job-definition problem.

Businesses need to ask what they are actually paying people to accomplish. Employees need to ask the same question about themselves.

If the majority of a role is repetitive production, automation will probably affect it significantly. If the role depends heavily on judgement, context, relationships, taste, problem framing or responsibility for outcomes, AI is more likely to become an additional capability.

The practical question is therefore not simply whether AI can do something.

It is whether that thing is the reason the role exists.

That distinction matters because technology tends to remove work before it removes responsibility. Someone still has to decide what should be done, why it should be done, whether the result is good enough and what happens next.

The people who adapt well may not be the ones who compete with AI at doing more tasks.

They may be the ones who become better at deciding which tasks should exist at all.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 03
Creative Teams Need Systems Too
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Creative work is often treated as something that should remain free from systems.

Introduce a process into a creative team and someone will eventually say: “This is creative work. Systems won't work here.”

That sounds reasonable until creative work has to happen repeatedly, across multiple people, clients, deadlines and formats.

A system does not have to tell a creative person what idea to have. It can simply make the environment around the idea more reliable.

There is an important difference between a system and a restriction.

A restriction says: this is the only way to do it.

A useful system says: here is how we make sure the important parts do not get lost.

Creative teams need systems for briefs, approvals, feedback, asset management, timelines, responsibilities and quality checks. Without them, creativity itself often becomes the thing paying the price.

The irony is that a lack of structure can reduce creative freedom.

When every project starts with uncertainty about who owns the brief, what the deadline is, who approves the work and what information is missing, the team spends creative energy solving operational problems.

Good systems remove some of that noise.

The idea behind systems as a “safety net” is useful here. A safety net does not tell a performer how to perform. It gives them room to take risks because failure does not automatically become chaos.

The same applies to creative teams.

A designer can experiment more freely when the brief is clear. A copywriter can challenge the obvious idea when there is enough time for exploration. A creative director can reject a weak execution when the review process is designed to catch it before it reaches the client.

The goal is not to make creative work predictable.

The goal is to make the conditions around creative work predictable enough for creativity to happen.

This matters even more as teams scale. What works when three people sit together in the same room can break down when the team grows, clients increase and work moves across multiple channels.

Creative freedom and operational discipline are not opposites.

In good teams, the system protects the space where creativity happens.

The system is not the handcuff. It is often what makes the room larger.

When Businesses Keep Going Because They Have Already Invested Too Much
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Businesses do not always continue with an idea because it is working.

Sometimes they continue because they have already invested too much in it.

Money is the obvious version. But sunk cost can also be time, effort, reputation, internal politics, technology, relationships or simply the emotional investment attached to a decision.

Once a business has spent months building a product, years developing a market or a significant budget on a campaign, stopping can feel like admitting failure.

That creates a subtle problem.

The original decision starts receiving protection from the people who made it.

Instead of asking “Is this still the right direction?”, the organisation starts asking “How can we make the direction we chose work?”

Those are very different questions.

A marketing campaign can face the same problem. If a company has invested heavily in a particular positioning, it may keep producing variations of the same message even after customer behaviour suggests that the positioning is not working.

A product team can keep adding features because abandoning the product would make previous development work feel wasted.

A business can continue paying for a channel because leaving it would force an uncomfortable conversation about the money already spent.

But past investment cannot be recovered by making a poor decision today.

The useful question is not how much has already been invested.

It is what the next unit of investment is likely to produce.

That does not mean businesses should abandon every difficult project at the first sign of resistance. Some ideas need time. Brand building takes time. New products often take time. Experiments can fail before the learning becomes useful.

The distinction is between persistence and attachment.

Persistence responds to evidence: the strategy is difficult, but there are signs that it is moving in the right direction.

Attachment responds to history: we have already come this far, so we must continue.

Good decision-making requires the ability to separate the two.

Sometimes the most responsible business decision is to continue.

Sometimes it is to change direction.

And sometimes the reason a decision feels impossible to change is simply that too much of the past is sitting inside it.

The Next Battle Between Instagram and YouTube May Be About Social, Not Video
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Instagram and YouTube are often compared as video platforms. That comparison misses an important difference.

The real competition may increasingly be about what happens around the video.

Instagram has built an unusually strong social layer around content. A reel does not always reach someone through the feed. It often arrives through a friend, a group chat, a direct message or a shared conversation.

That changes the role of content.

A piece of content can become a social object.

YouTube has enormous strength in video discovery, search, long-form viewing and Shorts. But the question is what happens when a platform that is already dominant in video begins strengthening the social behaviours surrounding it.

This matters to marketers because distribution is changing.

For years, the basic mental model was simple: create content, publish it, and hope the platform distributes it.

That is no longer enough.

People increasingly distribute content to one another.

A video may be discovered by one person but consumed by a group. A recommendation may travel through a private conversation before it becomes a public interaction. The original platform matters, but the social path the content takes can matter just as much.

This also changes how content should be evaluated.

Views remain useful. But they do not tell the whole story.

Is the content being shared? Is someone sending it to another person because it is useful, funny, relevant or surprisingly accurate? Does it start a conversation?

Those behaviours are harder to manufacture.

They also explain why some relatively simple pieces of content travel further than highly produced campaigns. The content gives people a reason to pass it on.

The platforms will continue to compete on formats, screens and features.

For brands, however, the more interesting question may be simpler:

What makes someone want to send this to another person?

That is a different creative brief from “How do we get more views?”

Most of Your Customers Aren't Ready to Buy Yet
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

A common marketing expectation is that communication should produce an immediate response.

See the ad. Visit the page. Make the purchase.

Real customer behaviour is rarely that clean.

People often encounter a brand long before they need the product. They may notice the name repeatedly without understanding what it does. They may recognise the logo before they ever become a customer. They may encounter the brand through a billboard, packaging, a conversation or a social post and move on.

Nothing appears to happen.

Then, months or years later, the need arrives.

At that point, familiarity can matter.

This is particularly visible in categories such as jewellery, textiles, healthcare, education, financial services and many other products that people do not purchase every week.

A brand can spend years becoming familiar before a customer has a reason to act.

That creates a tension between short-term marketing measurement and long-term brand building.

If every campaign is judged only by immediate enquiries or sales, activities that create familiarity can look inefficient. But the absence of an immediate transaction does not necessarily mean the communication had no effect.

The opposite extreme is also dangerous. Not every campaign can be justified as “brand building” simply because sales did not happen.

The useful distinction is between communication that creates memory and communication that creates action.

Both have a role.

A customer who already knows the brand may respond faster when the need appears. A customer who has never heard of it may need much more reassurance before buying.

This is why businesses should be careful with the expectation that marketing must behave like a vending machine.

Put money in. Get customers out.

Some marketing is closer to planting a memory.

It may be difficult to measure in the moment, but that does not make it irrelevant.

The challenge is knowing which kind of marketing a business is doing, what job it is supposed to perform, and over what timeframe that job should be judged.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 07
Digital Marketing Is Still Marketing
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Many digital marketing courses now lead with tools.

AI. Meta Ads. Google Ads. SEO. Analytics. Content platforms. Automation.

The tools matter. But knowing how to operate them is not the same as knowing how to market.

A driving school can teach someone how to use the accelerator, brake, indicator and mirrors. That does not mean the person understands how to drive in traffic.

Digital marketing has a similar problem.

A person can learn how to set up an ad campaign without understanding the customer. They can learn how to create a reel without understanding positioning. They can learn SEO without knowing what the business should be known for.

The result is a growing number of people who can operate marketing tools without necessarily understanding the marketing problem those tools are supposed to solve.

That distinction becomes even more important as tools become easier.

AI can generate content. Platforms can automate targeting. Dashboards can report performance. Templates can accelerate production.

The easier the tools become, the less useful tool knowledge is as a complete definition of marketing expertise.

The difficult part remains understanding people.

Why would someone choose this product?

What does the customer already believe?

What makes the offer credible?

What problem is actually being solved?

Why should this message matter now?

What happens after someone clicks?

Those are marketing questions. The platform is simply where some of the answers get executed.

This is not an argument against digital skills. Quite the opposite. Strong marketers need to understand the tools well enough to use them intelligently.

But the order matters.

Marketing should determine the role of the tools, not the other way around.

A business does not need “more digital marketing” simply because it is digital.

It needs better marketing, with digital channels used where they make sense.

The industry may keep changing its platforms and terminology.

The basic job remains remarkably familiar: understand people, understand the business, make a useful promise, communicate it clearly, and create a path for the customer to act.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 08
The Segmentation Blind Spot: What People Are Willing to Spend
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Marketing segmentation usually begins with variables that are easy to identify.

Age. Location. Profession. Income. Gender. Sometimes interests and lifestyle.

These variables are useful. But they can also create a false sense of understanding.

Two people can earn the same amount of money and have completely different ideas about what is worth paying for.

One may spend heavily on convenience and avoid expensive products. Another may spend very little on convenience but pay a premium for quality. One may be extremely price-sensitive about food but willing to spend on travel. Another may behave in exactly the opposite way.

Income tells a marketer something about capacity.

It does not necessarily tell them about willingness.

That distinction matters because people do not allocate money according to a universal formula. They allocate it according to priorities.

For one customer, time is expensive. Paying for delivery makes sense.

For another, money is the scarce resource. Waiting an hour is acceptable.

For one person, a premium product feels unnecessary.

For another, the same premium is evidence of quality.

This is where segmentation can become more useful.

Instead of asking only “How much can this customer spend?”, marketers can ask:

What does this person value?

What are they willing to spend money, time or effort to avoid?

What are they willing to pay more for?

What compromises are they comfortable making?

These questions can reveal opportunities that demographic segmentation misses.

They also affect positioning.

A convenience-led product should not necessarily compete on price. A premium product should not assume every high-income customer is automatically a buyer. A mass-market product can still attract customers who occasionally spend heavily when the perceived value is right.

The customer is not a spreadsheet row.

Their purchasing behaviour is shaped by priorities, context and perceived value.

Better segmentation does not always mean adding more demographic variables.

Sometimes it means asking a more useful question about the people already sitting inside the segment.

People Are Already Being Influenced. The Question Is by What.
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

“Why bother convincing people?”

It sounds like a reasonable response to advertising.

But people influence one another constantly.

A recommendation in a group chat. A restaurant someone mentions. A creator demonstrating a product. A colleague complaining about a service. A friend sharing a campaign. A family member recommending a store.

Most of this does not feel like advertising.

That is precisely why it matters.

A recent retail campaign illustrates the difference. Maharani Wedding Collections in Thodupuzha used a campaign built around the idea that people are already influencing one another. The message did not need to behave like a conventional sales advertisement. It could enter an existing social behaviour: people noticing, discussing and passing on opinions.

This is a useful reminder for marketers.

Consumers are not passive recipients of communication.

They are part of the distribution system.

A brand message can travel through people in ways the original campaign plan never anticipated. Sometimes the strongest media placement is not a billboard or an ad platform. It is a conversation.

That does not mean every campaign should try to become viral.

It means marketers should understand the social environment around the category.

What do people already talk about?

What do they share?

What do they complain about?

What do they recommend?

What kind of message would they naturally pass to someone else?

These questions can produce more interesting creative work than simply asking what content should be posted next.

The audience is not merely an audience.

They are also people talking to other people.

Marketing becomes more interesting when that is treated as part of the medium rather than an accidental side effect.



CASE STUDY | ARTICLE 10
Good Ideas Are Not Single-Use
Author: Ananthu Vasudev
Excerpt: A useful marketing idea does not always belong to one placement.

A campaign doesn’t need a new idea every time the placement changes.

We learned that properly on a project for Allen & Habour Opticals.

The starting point was a Malayalam ad on Zomato’s order-tracking page. The line — “വിശന്നിട്ട് കണ്ണ് കാണുന്നില്ലേ?” — took a familiar expression about hunger and not being able to “see” and pointed it at eyewear. It gave the brand a culturally familiar way into a moment that has nothing to do with conventional optical advertising.

The placement was part of the thinking. Someone on an order-tracking screen has already ordered. They’re waiting. The environment is temporary and the mood is specific. A generic optical ad could have sat there. The opportunity was to make the surrounding moment part of the idea.

Then a second requirement came in: an ad for a screen inside a restaurant.

The normal response is to treat that as a new brief. New location, new screen, new context, new concept.

We didn’t.

## What we noticed

Creative teams fall into one of two traps.

The first is producing a completely new idea for every placement. That creates volume, not coherence — and pressure to manufacture novelty when a strong idea already exists.

The second is taking the same asset and resizing it for every channel.

Neither is satisfying. The useful middle ground is to separate the idea from its execution. The idea stays. The expression changes.

## The decision

We carried the underlying thought forward instead of restarting the strategic process.

The customer’s physical situation had changed, so the execution could change with it. What stayed constant was the connection between the language, the moment and the category.

## The execution

On Zomato, the food-ordering context was part of the idea. On the restaurant screen, the same thought moved into a different physical environment.

This wasn’t a production shortcut. It took judgement about what could stay, what had to change, and how much of the setup the new placement supplied on its own.

## What happened

The idea travelled. It moved from a food-delivery app to a restaurant wall without needing to be reinvented.

We won’t put impressions, clicks or sales next to that, because we don’t have verified numbers for this work — and we’d rather leave a gap than fill it with something that looks like proof.

## What didn’t work

Nothing failed here, and we’re not going to invent a setback to make the story feel balanced.

There was a real risk, though. Reuse the whole ad mechanically and repetition becomes obvious fast. What should travel is the insight — not every pixel of the execution.

## What changed

We stopped thinking in individual advertisements and started thinking in idea systems.

The question moved from “What’s the new ad for this placement?” to “What can this idea do in this context?”

## What we learned

Good ideas are not single-use.

The strongest ones survive a change of screen, location, audience moment or format. That doesn’t mean every execution should look the same. It means the thought is strong enough to be interpreted again.

The most efficient creative system isn’t the one that produces the most ideas.

It’s the one that knows which ideas deserve to travel.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 11
A Campaign You Don't Like Can Still Be a Good Campaign
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Marketing professionals are exposed to thousands of campaigns. That makes personal taste almost impossible to separate from professional judgement.

But the two are not the same.

Not liking an advertisement does not automatically make it ineffective.

The distinction becomes obvious when considering everyday consumer choices. One person may happily pay for food delivery because convenience is valuable to them. Another may prefer driving to the restaurant because saving money matters more.

The product is the same. The value equation is different.

Advertising works inside the same reality.

A campaign may be designed for an audience, a positioning or a behaviour that the marketer personally does not share.

That is not a problem.

The problem begins when personal preference becomes the evaluation criteria.

A marketer may dislike a visual style because it feels loud. The target audience may find it energetic and memorable.

A marketer may dislike an offer because they would never use it. The intended customer may see it as exactly what they need.

A marketer may think a campaign is boring because they have seen hundreds of advertisements. The customer may be encountering the category for the first time.

Good marketing judgement therefore requires distance from personal taste.

The better questions are:

Who is this for?

What is it trying to make them think, feel or do?

Does the creative fit the positioning?

Does it communicate clearly?

Is the message relevant to the audience?

And, where evidence exists, did it produce the intended outcome?

That does not mean every campaign should be defended.

Bad strategy is still bad strategy. Weak creative can still be weak creative.

But “I don't like it” is not enough evidence.

A marketing professional's job is not to design for themselves.

It is to understand the customer well enough to judge work from the customer's point of view.

Sometimes the most useful sentence in a creative review is not “I like it.”

It is “I understand why this might work for them.”



CASE STUDY | ARTICLE 12
Designing for Context: The Malayalam Ad on Zomato's Order-Tracking Page
Author: Ananthu Vasudev
Excerpt: A media placement can be bought as inventory. Or it can be treated as part of the idea.

A media placement can be bought as inventory.

Or it can be treated as part of the idea.

The Malayalam ad we built for Allen & Habour Opticals on Zomato’s order-tracking page is worth writing up because the second approach turned out to be the more useful one.

## The situation

Zomato opened its order-tracking screen to advertisers. For an optical brand, the obvious move was a standard display ad carrying a product message.

But the placement came with a specific context.

The customer had already ordered. They were waiting. Everything about that screen is tied to hunger, anticipation and a very familiar behaviour.

For a Malayalam-speaking audience, that added another layer.

## The problem

The job was not to make a Malayalam ad.

It was to make an ad that felt like it belonged where it appeared.

A translated optical ad can use perfectly correct Malayalam and still feel completely disconnected from what the customer is doing in that moment.

The better question was: what can an eyewear brand say to someone who is currently waiting for food?

## What we noticed

Context is more than demographic relevance.

A food-delivery app is obviously relevant to food. That doesn’t make every food-adjacent ad good.

The layer that matters is behaviour. The customer has ordered. They’re waiting. They’re staring at the screen. They’re in a short, specific state of anticipation.

That makes the environment nothing like a billboard, a newspaper or a social feed.

## The decision

Make the placement itself part of the message.

“വിശന്നിട്ട് കണ്ണ് കാണുന്നില്ലേ?” connects a familiar expression about hunger and “not being able to see” to the eyewear category.

So the decision was less about localisation and more about using language and context as creative material.

## The execution

Three elements: a Malayalam-speaking audience, a food-ordering moment, an eyewear proposition.

None of them is complicated on its own. The strength came from putting them together at the right moment.

## What happened

The idea held up well enough to be reused later on a restaurant screen — a different physical environment, same underlying thought. We wrote that up separately in Good Ideas Are Not Single-Use.

We don’t have verified impressions, click-throughs, enquiries or sales for this placement, so we’re not going to claim a commercial result the work doesn’t establish.

## What didn’t work

No route was rejected on this one.

But there was a clear risk: contextual advertising can get too clever. If people have to decode the relationship between the placement, the language and the brand, the context stops helping and starts costing.

The connection has to land in a second.

## What changed

The relationship between media and creative.

Media doesn’t have to be the container and creative the contents. They can shape each other. A placement tells the creative team something about the customer’s state of mind. A location supplies the setup. A language supplies the cultural reference.

## What we learned

When you look at a new medium, “How many people can we reach?” is not the only useful question.

Try: “What does this environment let us say that another one doesn’t?”

Sometimes the answer is a much better brief.

A Logo Does Not Become a Brand Just Because There Is Space for It
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Branding often becomes a matter of personal preference.

A business owner likes one logo. A designer recommends another. A marketing team suggests a third direction.

The discussion can quickly become subjective.

But a brand identity is not designed only for the people inside the company.

This becomes particularly visible on sports jerseys.

A jersey contains a limited amount of visual space. Teams, sponsors and manufacturers compete for attention. Logos have to coexist with colours, typography, symbols and the visual identity of the team itself.

The question is not simply whether a logo looks good.

It is whether it communicates the brand effectively in that environment.

A common mistake in branding is to treat approval from the business owner as the finish line.

The owner may prefer a certain colour. The founder may like a particular typeface. The team may be emotionally attached to an old symbol.

Those preferences are real, but they are not the same as brand strategy.

The customer does not see the logo with the same internal history.

They see it as one signal among many.

That means branding decisions need to consider recognition, distinctiveness, relevance, readability, context and consistency.

A logo that looks excellent on a presentation slide may fail when printed small.

A colour combination that feels premium in isolation may disappear against another brand's visual environment.

A design that the founder loves may communicate a completely different personality to the intended audience.

This is why branding requires a degree of distance.

The question is not “Do we like it?”

It is “What does this make people think?”

Personal preference can be part of the discussion.

It should not be the strategy.

A brand exists in the minds of customers, not inside the approval meeting.

The logo is one of the ways that brand enters their world.

That makes the audience the final context for the decision.



CASE STUDY | ARTICLE 14
When a Marketing Idea Moves Faster Than the Campaign Calendar
Author: Ananthu Vasudev
Excerpt: Some of the strongest campaign ideas do not begin months in advance.

Some campaigns are built months ahead.

Others exist because someone noticed a moment was about to disappear.

The Women’s Day campaign for Malabar Village Luxe in Kottayam is the second kind.

## The situation

Women’s Day is a familiar marketing moment. For most brands that means communication prepared weeks in advance, reviewed in stages, and published somewhere around March 8.

Planning is useful. It also puts distance between an opportunity and the decision to act on it.

For Malabar Village Luxe, the starting point was much closer to the moment.

## The problem

The brief wasn’t “do something for Women’s Day.” That produces a calendar topic, not an idea.

The real challenge was to find something relevant enough to the brand and the occasion that it deserved to exist at all.

And there was a second constraint: time. The idea surfaced on the evening of March 5. A conventional campaign process would have made the window too slow to use.

## What we noticed

Speed on its own isn’t the interesting part.

The interesting part is the relationship between the size of the idea and the size of the process wrapped around it.

A major rebrand needs scrutiny. A large media campaign needs planning. A small, cultural-moment execution needs momentum.

Run all three through the same machinery and the team gets slower without getting better.

## The decision

We developed the concept quickly instead of dropping it into a long campaign cycle.

The internal discussion happened on March 5. A proposal went to the client, who responded almost immediately.

The decision wasn’t simply to move fast. It was to keep the process proportionate to the opportunity.

## The execution

A small Women’s Day initiative that moved from internal discussion to client proposal to approval in a matter of hours.

We’re not adding a production log, final assets or a media plan here, because this piece is about the decision, not the deliverables.

## What happened

The campaign happened because the decision-making matched the scale and timing of the idea.

That’s different from saying every process should be faster. The principle is that the process should fit the problem.

## What didn’t work

Nothing was rejected or reworked on this one.

There is an operational risk worth naming, though: speed can become an excuse for weak thinking. Moving quickly doesn’t remove the need for a clear idea, brand fit or audience relevance. It removes the time you’d normally spend checking for them — so the checks have to be faster, not absent.

## What changed

An operational idea that’s easy to underrate: speed is a creative capability.

A team that can notice a relevant moment, form a useful thought, make a decision and execute without friction has access to opportunities slower organisations will miss.

## What we learned

Moment marketing is usually treated as a creative problem.

It’s also an organisational one.

The question isn’t only whether a brand can think of something interesting. It’s whether the business can make a good decision while the opportunity still exists.

Sometimes the advantage isn’t having the best idea in the room.

It’s being able to do something with it before the room moves on.

Sometimes the Billboard Next to Yours Is Part of Your Campaign
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Outdoor advertising is usually planned as if every billboard exists independently.

But on a road, customers do not see advertisements one at a time.

They see them together.

That creates a possibility that is still underused in many markets: contextual or reactive outdoor advertising, where one brand's message responds to another brand, a cultural event or something happening around it.

International advertising has produced memorable examples of this kind of public conversation. Competitors such as Pepsi and Coca-Cola, McDonald's and Burger King, and Mercedes and BMW have used outdoor media to create playful exchanges.

The appeal is obvious.

A billboard becomes more than a message from one brand. It becomes part of a conversation taking place in public.

Kerala's outdoor advertising environment has plenty of visual competition, but direct contextual responses are less common.

That makes the opportunity interesting.

A brand could respond to a competitor's campaign.

A retailer could react to a cultural moment.

Two businesses could create a planned exchange.

A billboard could even use the physical location around it as part of the message.

The important point is that the creative idea does not have to live entirely inside the artwork.

It can live in the relationship between two pieces of communication.

This is a useful way to think about OOH.

The medium is physical. The audience is moving. The context is visible. The surrounding advertisements are part of the environment.

A clever campaign can use all of that.

The challenge is restraint.

Reactive advertising can easily become noise or forced cleverness. A response is interesting only when the relationship is clear enough for people to understand it.

But when the context works, the billboard becomes something more than a static placement.

It becomes part of the conversation happening on the road.

“AI-Free” Is Not a Creative Strategy
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

“AI-free content.”

“100% human-made.”

“No ChatGPT.”

These labels are increasingly used as signals of originality.

But not using a tool does not automatically make the work more original.

A person can produce a completely ordinary piece of content without AI. Another person can use AI as one part of a thoughtful creative process and produce something genuinely distinctive.

The important variable is not whether AI was involved.

It is what the person did with the tool.

Every creative industry has always used tools. Cameras did not make photographers less creative. Editing software did not make filmmakers less creative. Design software did not eliminate designers.

The tool changes what is possible.

It does not decide what should be made.

AI is similar.

It can help generate options, challenge assumptions, restructure information, explore directions or accelerate repetitive work. It can also produce bland, repetitive material when used without judgement.

The difference comes from the human decision-making around the output.

What gets accepted?

What gets rejected?

What gets rewritten?

What context is added?

What does the creator know about the audience that the tool does not?

Those questions matter more than the badge attached to the process.

There is also a practical problem with treating “AI-free” as a quality signal.

Customers generally do not buy a product because the company avoided a particular tool.

They care whether the result is useful, relevant, trustworthy, distinctive or enjoyable.

A campaign does not become better because its production process was difficult.

Difficulty is not a creative metric.

Originality comes from the thinking.

Sometimes that thinking will use AI.

Sometimes it will not.

Neither choice is inherently virtuous.

The useful question is simpler:

Did the tool help produce better work, or did it get in the way?

That is a much more interesting standard than whether the work came with an “AI-free” certificate.

AI Is Becoming a Team Tool, Not Just an Individual Tool
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

AI is often imagined as a one-person interaction: one employee, one prompt, one answer.

That model is changing.

Collaborative AI features make it possible for teams to work with the same conversation, review outputs together and improve the result through discussion.

That matters because good AI output rarely comes from a single perfect prompt.

It usually comes through iteration.

Someone asks a question.

Someone notices that the response is missing context.

Another person changes the wording.

Someone adds a business constraint.

Another person challenges the assumption.

The output improves because the thinking improves.

This is closer to how teams already work.

A good strategy meeting does not depend on one person asking the perfect question. It depends on multiple people bringing context, disagreement, examples and judgement.

AI can become part of that process.

The interesting shift is therefore not simply “AI can answer questions.”

It is that AI can become a shared workspace for thinking.

For marketing teams, this could mean developing campaign directions together, challenging positioning, exploring customer objections, summarising research or testing different versions of an idea.

For operations teams, it could mean examining a process, identifying gaps and documenting alternatives.

For sales teams, it could mean analysing conversations and building better responses.

But collaboration does not remove the need for human judgement.

If anything, it increases it.

A team can generate ten possible directions quickly. It still needs to decide which one deserves attention.

The value of collaborative AI is therefore less about replacing discussion and more about making discussion more productive.

The best output may still come from a messy conversation.

AI simply gives the conversation another participant.

The Best Business Automation May Not Need New Software
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

One of the smartest low-cost examples of sales automation can look almost too simple to count as automation.

A small tyre shop used a basic customer-follow-up mechanism: after a sale, the customer was asked to save the shop's contact number and send a simple message or sticker when the time came for the next service or tyre-related need.

The process was not built on an expensive CRM.

It did not require a complicated chatbot.

It simply created a trigger that made it easier for the business to re-enter the customer's attention at the right time.

That is the interesting part.

Businesses often approach automation by asking which software they should buy.

A better starting question is:

Where are we repeatedly losing time, information or follow-up?

Automation is useful when it removes friction from a repeatable process.

The process does not have to be technologically impressive.

A reminder can be automation.

A template can be automation.

A structured WhatsApp workflow can be automation.

A spreadsheet connected to a recurring process can be automation.

The technology matters less than the problem being solved.

For a tyre shop, customer follow-up has a natural time dimension. People may not need the service every week. Without a reminder, the business may disappear from the customer's attention until the customer notices the need again.

A small system can keep the relationship alive.

This is particularly relevant to small businesses because the cost of sophisticated software can become a distraction. The business may spend more time configuring the system than solving the original problem.

Good automation starts small.

Identify a repeated task.

Find the point where human memory or manual effort creates friction.

Create a simple trigger.

Measure whether the process actually improves.

Then make it more sophisticated only if the problem justifies it.

The goal of automation is not to look automated.

It is to make the business work better.

A Good Product Can Become Unpopular Because of How It Is Sold
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Insurance is a useful example of a marketing problem that is not really a product problem.

The underlying value is easy to understand: financial protection when something goes wrong.

Yet insurance has developed a complicated reputation in India.

Part of that history comes from how the product was sold.

Insurance was often associated with aggressive sales practices, repeated calls, personal networks and pressure to buy. In many cases, the product conversation became less about protection and more about completing a sale.

That matters because marketing does not merely communicate products.

It can shape how categories are perceived.

When customers repeatedly encounter a category through uncomfortable sales experiences, the category itself can become associated with those experiences.

The result is a strange situation.

A useful product can become something people avoid discussing.

This is a broader lesson for businesses.

Customer perception is influenced not only by the product and advertising but also by the way the company reaches people.

A good campaign cannot completely compensate for a bad sales experience.

A strong product cannot fully escape a poor customer journey.

And repeated short-term sales tactics can damage long-term category trust.

This is why marketing, sales and customer experience should not be treated as unrelated departments.

They are different parts of the same promise.

If marketing says “we are here to protect you” and the sales process feels like pressure, the customer receives two conflicting messages.

The same principle applies outside insurance.

Education, healthcare, financial services, real estate and professional services all depend heavily on trust.

The way the offer is introduced becomes part of the offer.

Sometimes the marketing problem is not that people need more persuasion.

They may need a better experience of the category.

Sales Is Not the Last Department of a Business
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Sales is often treated as the final stage of business.

Marketing creates awareness.

The product creates value.

Sales converts the customer.

Then the process moves on.

That model is too narrow.

Sales is one of the places where a business discovers what the market actually thinks.

Customers reveal objections.

They compare alternatives.

They ask questions the marketing team did not anticipate.

They reject offers.

They negotiate.

They explain what they value.

All of that information is valuable.

Yet many organisations separate sales from the teams making decisions about marketing, product and customer experience.

That creates a feedback problem.

Marketing may continue communicating a message that sales teams know customers do not understand.

Product teams may build features that customers rarely ask for.

Leadership may assume the market is price-sensitive when the actual objection is trust.

Salespeople hear these things first.

That makes sales more than a conversion function.

It is a market intelligence function.

This is also why sales skills are useful far beyond sales roles.

Entrepreneurs sell.

Managers sell ideas internally.

Recruiters sell opportunities.

Marketers sell propositions.

Consultants sell recommendations.

Even operations teams often need to convince other people to change behaviour.

The underlying skill is not persuasion for its own sake.

It is understanding what another person needs in order to make a decision.

Businesses become stronger when sales is connected to the rest of the organisation.

Marketing should know what objections sales hears.

Sales should understand what the brand promises.

Product should know why customers hesitate.

Leadership should listen to all three.

A business does not really have a marketing problem, sales problem and product problem sitting in separate boxes.

It has one customer.

The departments simply see different parts of that customer's decision.

Customer Experience Starts Before the Product Does
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

A product showroom is usually evaluated by the product on display.

But customers experience a business before they touch the product.

A visit to River's showroom in Kochi is a useful example.

The experience began before the scooter itself became the focus. The security guard helped with parking. The interaction was calm and welcoming. The environment felt closer to a contemporary technology or design company than a conventional automobile showroom.

That matters because customers do not experience businesses in departmental categories.

They experience a sequence.

Arrival.

Parking.

Greeting.

Navigation.

Conversation.

Product interaction.

Payment.

Follow-up.

Every small interaction contributes to the perception of the brand.

A business can spend heavily on branding and advertising and then lose some of that value through a poor first interaction.

The reverse is also true.

A thoughtful experience can strengthen the positioning created by the marketing.

This is particularly important for categories where the product itself is difficult to differentiate.

Electric vehicles provide an interesting example. Specifications can be compared. Prices can be compared. Features can be listed.

Experience is harder to compare on a spreadsheet.

How does the brand make a customer feel?

Does the environment create confidence?

Do employees behave like the brand claims to behave?

Does the process feel modern, easy and considered?

These details become part of the product experience.

Customer experience therefore should not be treated as a customer-support problem that begins after purchase.

It begins the moment someone encounters the business.

Sometimes that moment is an advertisement.

Sometimes it is a Google search.

Sometimes it is a security guard helping someone park.

The customer does not separate these interactions.

Neither should the brand.



PERSPECTIVE | ARTICLE 22
Why Should Businesses Be the Only Ones Being Rated?
Author: Ananthu Vasudev

Businesses are increasingly measured through ratings.

Google reviews.

Restaurant ratings.

Marketplace reviews.

Delivery feedback.

Service scores.

The logic is useful. Customers need a way to communicate their experience and businesses need feedback.

But the system is usually one-directional.

The customer rates the business.

The business rarely gets an equivalent way to record what happened from its side.

This becomes particularly relevant in service businesses and platforms where the interaction is between two people.

A delivery worker can be rated by a customer.

A freelancer can be reviewed by a client.

A restaurant can be reviewed by a diner.

But difficult behaviour from the customer may disappear once the transaction is complete.

A mutual rating system could create a different kind of accountability.

That does not mean businesses should be given a tool to punish customers for legitimate complaints. Ratings can be abused by either side. Any system would need clear rules, privacy safeguards and protection against retaliation.

The broader idea is about symmetry.

If platforms want ratings to represent trust, they should consider whether trust is a two-way relationship.

A customer is not automatically right because they are paying.

Payment buys a service.

It does not buy ownership of the person providing it.

That distinction matters in an economy increasingly built around service workers, freelancers, small businesses and platform-based work.

Customer experience should be protected.

So should worker dignity.

A better review system may not be about giving businesses more power.

It may simply recognise that every transaction involves at least two sides.
